RepresentDC — Internal Strategy Doc

Getting people from "informed" to "in the fight"

An audit of where our calls-to-action are buried, and a proposed information architecture that separates the persuasion front-door from the action destinations — organized around four visitor mindsets.

DRAFT — July 2026 — for discussion, not yet built

01The problem, concretely

The persuasion strategy on representdc.org works — loss framing, concrete facts, values framing, then the statehood solution. But the site has no real "do something" destination. Every action we've built is real and useful; it's just buried under content that's designed to inform, not act.

Talking points are 3 clicks deep. Home page → click a category card to expand it (1) → read the description → click the "💬 Talking Points" button (2) → modal opens (3) → then choose an audience tab and a length. A volunteer who already knows they want to post something today has to first sit through a history lesson to find it.
News is trapped inside the Bill Tracker. "Recent Activity" and the news feed only render on billtracker.representdc.org, above a wall of filters and 96 bills. There's no lightweight "what happened this week" page to send a casual follower to.
There is no "actions and events" surface at all. Anyone ready to volunteer, attend something, or take an offline step has nowhere to go — this is a genuine content gap, not just an IA problem.
One homepage tries to serve everyone at once. A returning activist and a first-time visitor scroll through the exact same "why statehood matters" narrative. The page has no memory of who's reading it.

02The governing idea

Split the site into two jobs it currently does at once:

Job 1Persuade & orient

The homepage's job is to help a stranger understand the problem fast, and figure out which of four doors fits them. It stays loss-framed, evidence-first, soft-CTA — exactly the voice already documented in STRATEGY.md.

Job 2Equip & mobilize

A separate, well-lit set of destinations does the "now go do something" job: talking points, news, events. These can be blunter and faster — no re-litigating why it matters, just tools.

Right now Job 2 is smuggled inside Job 1's content (a talking-points modal hidden inside a persuasion card). Pulling it out doesn't mean removing it from context — it means giving it its own front door and leaving a visible, low-friction pointer to it everywhere it's contextually relevant.

04Homepage: how self-identification actually works

Recommended pattern — a persuasion-first hero with an early, honest fork, not a quiz gate:

Hero: "Congress is blocking D.C. laws right now" + 0 senators / 96 bills stat
↓ scroll
"Where do you want to start?" — 4-card persona picker
New to D.C. → Explainer path
Curious → News + ways to help
Questioning → Myths & FAQ
Activist → Action hub
↓ for anyone who keeps scrolling instead of picking
Full narrative continues underneath (facts → values → solution) — the existing homepage content, now framed as the "New to D.C." default path

Why not a hard quiz/gate: a skeptical visitor (persona 3) who is forced to click through a quiz before getting an answer will bounce. Placing the fork after the hook keeps the proven hero, gives self-identifying visitors a fast lane immediately, and still lets a passive scroller fall through to the full persuasion narrative by default — nobody is forced to choose.

The picker itself is a strip of 4 cards (icon, one line of framing copy, one link) — not a modal, not a redirect with no context. Each card's copy is written to that persona ("Not sure D.C. isn't already a state? Start here.") so people recognize themselves.

05Where should "Take Action" live? — three options

You flagged this as the open question. Laying out the real tradeoff:

AOne dedicated Action Hub page

Single page (e.g. /take-action) holding all three tools: talking points browser, news feed, events/actions list — as tabs or stacked sections. Homepage and every persona path link to it once they're ready to act.

Pro: one URL to promote everywhere (social bio, emails, footer); easy to maintain; matches how an activist actually behaves — they want the whole toolbox, not one piece at a time.
Con: talking points, news, and events are pretty different tasks (copy text vs. skim headlines vs. RSVP) — mashing them into one page risks becoming a junk drawer if not organized carefully.

BNo hub — actions distributed contextually

No standalone destination. Talking points surface at the bottom of the category cards where they already live (just promoted, not hidden); news lives as its own top-level page; events get their own page. Each persona path ends in whichever action fits it.

Pro: action stays attached to the context that motivates it (you just read about the criminal-code override → here's the talking point for it).
Con: no single place to point a volunteer who wants "everything I can do" in one visit; harder to promote one link.

Decided ✓C — Hybrid: sitewide action affordance + a real hub

Two things, not one: (1) a persistent, lightweight "Take Action" entry in the top nav on every page (not just the homepage) with a small dropdown/menu — Talking Points · Latest News · Actions & Events — so it's never more than one click away from anywhere on the site, including mid-scroll on a category card. (2) a real hub page at that link's destination, organized as three clearly separated sections (not tabs merged into mush) so each keeps its own scan pattern: talking points as a browsable, filterable card grid; news as a reverse-chron feed; events as a simple upcoming/past list.

This gets you A's "one promotable URL" and B's "never buried" without picking one and losing the other. Category cards on the homepage/explainer path can also keep a contextual "Talking points for this →" link that deep-links into the hub's relevant filter — best of both without duplicating content.

The empty state (when there's nothing scheduled)

Public statehood events aren't constant, and the pattern across partner sites (LWVDC's own calendar included) is a bare "no events found" — a dead end that undercuts the "momentum, not a pipe dream" framing from STRATEGY.md §5. Proposed alternative: never show a blank state. When no events are listed, the same slot instead surfaces one of two positive redirects:

  • Meet the movement — a stakeholder map / key-partners list (content you'll provide) introducing the orgs already doing this work, so a visitor still leaves with something concrete instead of "check back later."
  • Something to do right now — a direct link back to the Take Action hub (talking points, news) so the moment isn't wasted even without an event to plug into.

Both keep the empty slot "in motion" rather than reading as abandonment — small thing, but consistent with not looking like every other statehood site's dead calendar page.

The "meet the movement" stakeholder map

"Who's actually working on this?" is one of the most common questions a curious or activist visitor will have — and it's genuinely hard to answer well. There isn't one org, there's a layered coalition: a small set of core groups doing the day-to-day organizing and policy work, sitting inside a much wider coalition (100+ organizations have co-signed statehood letters to Congress at once). Trying to render all of it as one flat map would be unreadable and would misrepresent the structure.

Proposed two-tier treatment:

Tier 1Core groups (the map itself)

A small, labeled set — realistically 5–8 entries — each with a one-line "what they do" so a visitor understands the division of labor, not just a logo wall. Named orgs to seed this with:

  • DC Vote — voting-rights research & advocacy org; source for the myths content in §7
  • League of Women Voters of DC — nonpartisan civic org; hosts the statehood-game events in this section
  • Free DC — home-rule & anti-interference advocacy
  • DC Democratic Party Statehood Committee — party-affiliated statehood organizing (flag per guardrail §7: label this one's party affiliation plainly and explicitly, since it's a fact about the org, not a RepresentDC stance)
  • Neighbors United for DC Statehood — grassroots/neighborhood organizing; source for FAQ content in §7
  • DC Statehood Pledge — candidate accountability tool (related to RepresentDC's own candidate/pledge product — worth a direct cross-link)
  • Students for DC Statehood — student organizing
  • New Columbia Statehood Commission — official D.C. government body created to advance statehood

Tier 2The wider coalition

Together for DC is the connective coalition layer — its steering committee alone lists 16 organizations (Center for Economic Democracy, DC Appleseed, DC Fiscal Policy Institute, DC Jobs With Justice, DC Justice Lab, DC Chapter NOW, Fair Budget Coalition, Miriam's Kitchen, National LGBTQ Task Force, Public Citizen, SPACEs in Action, Taskforce for Democracy, and others, alongside several Tier-1 orgs above). 07Statehood-questioning path: myths & FAQ

From DC Vote — "Six Popular Myths about the District of Columbia"

MythThe rebuttal
"Most D.C. residents work for the government or military"Federal workers are a small slice of the population — D.C. is home to roughly 660,000 people of all occupations, most of whom have lived there 20+ years, across 120 distinct neighborhoods.
"D.C. doesn't pay U.S. taxes but gets lots of federal money"D.C. residents pay federal income tax at the highest per-capita rate in the country, plus local "statelike" taxes that Congress must approve. Eight states receive more federal funding as a share of their budget than D.C. does.
"D.C. residents have the same rights as other Americans"They pay federal taxes, serve in the military, and sit on federal juries — but have no voting representation in Congress, and Congress must approve every local law and budget, unlike any state.
"Proximity to the Capitol gives D.C. residents influence"Proximity isn't access — most members of Congress know their own home districts, not local D.C. issues.
"D.C. is too small to have representation"D.C. is 63 square miles with a larger population than Wyoming or Vermont — and every state gets equal Senate representation regardless of size.
"D.C. is treated differently because it belongs to all Americans"The National Mall/federal core is a small part of the District; the rest is 120 neighborhoods tourists never see. Eight states have a higher share of federal land ownership than D.C. does.
Source: DC Vote, "Six Popular Myths about the District of Columbia." Flag per guardrail §9: the population figure (~660,000) and per-capita tax claims read as dated — confirm current figures before publishing (D.C.'s 2026 population and current per-capita tax-rate rankings) and date-stamp this section.

From Neighbors United for DC Statehood (the51st.org) — FAQ selections

QuestionThe answer
Isn't D.C. statehood unconstitutional?No — the Constitution sets an upper size limit on the federal district (100 sq. mi.) but no lower limit, and doesn't fix its location. A shrunken federal district (Mall, Capitol, White House, military sites) could remain while the rest becomes a state.
Isn't D.C. too small / too subsidized to be a state?D.C. has more residents than Wyoming or Vermont and pays more federal tax than 22 states, by this source's figures. Roughly a quarter of its budget comes from federal funds — on par with or less than several states.
Do D.C. residents actually want statehood?86% of D.C. voters approved a 2016 statehood referendum, which also set the state's proposed name, borders, and constitution.
Is statehood actually achievable, or just a slogan?The U.S. has admitted a new state 37 times since the original 13 — a plain congressional majority vote plus a presidential signature, no constitutional amendment required.
Source: Neighbors United for DC Statehood, the51st.org/faqs. Flag per guardrail §9: some cited figures there (federal-funds comparisons) trace to 2008 Census data — re-verify against current numbers before reuse, and date both the claim and the "last verified" check.

That's off-voice for RepresentDC regardless of source — leave it out, or reframe generically (e.g. "corruption exists at every level of government, in every state") the way DC Vote's material does.

09Sitewide guardrails

Cross-cutting rules that apply to every page and persona path, not just this redesign — worth baking into how we write and build going forward:

GuardrailWhat it means in practice
Credit the movementOpenly and frequently credit other statehood organizations, researchers, and advocates whose work we reference or build on — never present others' arguments or research as originating with RepresentDC. Applies to talking points, hypocrisy research, myths content, and news items alike.
Source everythingEvery factual claim gets a visible, clickable source — not just in the internal framing-guide doc, but surfaced to visitors (a link or footnote, not just an internal citation). This is what makes the "persuadables" data-driven approach credible instead of assertion.
Date time-sensitive materialAnything that can go stale — bill statuses, "as of" stats, news items, event listings, even talking points tied to a specific hearing or vote — carries a visible date. A talking point copied from a 2025 hearing without a date reads as current and can misinform.
Match tone to the page's audienceReconfirms §3/§7: the explainer path, activist hub, and questioning-persona FAQ are allowed different registers (patient/efficient/clipped) — but all stay within the non-partisan, evidence-first voice. Check tone deliberately per page, not just per persona.
Don't assume prior knowledgeMany visitors are from outside D.C. and won't know things D.C. residents take for granted (what home rule is, that D.C. isn't a state, that residents pay federal taxes). Never reference local context (a neighborhood, a local official, "the Council") without a one-line gloss — this is squarely the "New to D.C." persona's whole reason for existing, but it should hold on every path.
Differentiate with surprise, not just more contentThe statehood-explainer space is crowded. Look for chances to add graphics, interactive bits, and light wit instead of another wall of text — e.g. a mythbusting quiz/game for the questioning persona, a "compare D.C. to your state" calculator, or surprising trivia (historical Republican supporters of D.C. statehood, past bipartisan votes). Not required on every page, but should be a real design goal, especially on the questioning and curious paths where a plain FAQ risks blending into every other advocacy site.
No party affiliation, ever, unless the content itself requires itReconfirms existing STRATEGY.md rule sitewide: never imply RepresentDC favors a party. The one carved-out exception is the candidate/statehood pledge tool, where a candidate's own party is shown as a neutral data badge because it's a fact about the candidate, not a stance we're taking.
