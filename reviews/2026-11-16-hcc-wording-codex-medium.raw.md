1. **(a) MATERIAL · OVERSTATED · lines 87, 107**

   “Counted only on days Congress is in session” is misleading. Weekends and holidays are excluded even during a session; weekdays during short adjournments can count. The recess exclusion applies when **neither House** sits because of a qualifying break. This is a statutory calendar, not a count of sitting days. [D.C. Code § 1-206.02(c)(1)](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/1-206.02)

   Suggested phrase: **“Excluding weekends, holidays, and certain congressional recesses.”**

2. **(b) MATERIAL · WRONG as an unconditional statement · lines 108–109**

   “Joint resolution” is correct. “The law never takes effect” requires enactment of that resolution within the review period. If both Houses pass it and transmit it to the President within the period, but it becomes law afterward, it **repeals** the D.C. act on that later date. [D.C. Code § 1-206.02(c)(1)–(2)](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/1-206.02)

   Presidential signature is also not the only route: enactment can occur through a veto override or without signature. [CRS R47927, “Congress”](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R47927.html#_Toc)

   Suggested wording: **“Congress can block the law by passing a joint resolution that becomes federal law before the review period ends. That usually requires the President’s signature, but Congress can override a veto. A resolution passed and sent to the President before the deadline can also become law afterward, repealing the D.C. law.”**

3. **(c) MATERIAL · OVERSTATED · lines 104–110**

   The emergency-law claim itself is **ACCURATE**: emergency acts can take effect immediately and last no more than 90 days. Call them “emergency laws”; “temporary legislation” is a separate category that does undergo congressional review. [D.C. Code § 1-204.12(a), including editor’s notes](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/1-204.12)

   However, “the exception” incorrectly makes the surrounding rule exhaustive. Section 1-206.02(c)(1) also excludes specified budget submissions and proposed charter amendments from its ordinary process. Charter amendments have a separate **35-day review**, rather than escaping congressional oversight. The local budget now follows congressional review under budget autonomy. [D.C. Code § 1-203.03](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/1-203.03), [§ 1-204.46](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/1-204.46)

   Suggested heading: **“Most local laws face congressional review.”**

   Suggested copy: **“Emergency laws can take effect immediately and last up to 90 days. Budget legislation and charter amendments have additional rules.”**

4. **(d) MATERIAL · WRONG as a categorical description · lines 131–136**

   The Local Budget Autonomy Amendment Act of 2012, enacted in 2013, allows the local budget to take effect after congressional review. D.C. has followed that process since 2016. Congress nevertheless continues approving, modifying, or disapproving the budget through appropriations; CRS also records the legal disagreement over autonomy. The word **“only”** erases this distinction. [CRS R49497, “Local Budget Autonomy”](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R49497.html), [D.C. Code § 1-204.46](https://code.dccouncil.gov/us/dc/council/code/sections/1-204.46)

   Suggested heading: **“Congress retains power over D.C.’s budget.”**

   Suggested copy: **“Under D.C.’s budget-autonomy law, the local budget can take effect after congressional review. Congress still uses federal funding bills to approve or change the budget and restrict local spending. Federal payments require congressional appropriations.”**
